The ChaiCart
Cloud Workshop

A two-day, game-based workshop for 120 engineering students (5th and 7th semester) in 24 teams of five. On Day 1 they found a cloud startup; on Day 2 they keep it alive through outages, bill shocks and a murder mystery.

How the workshop works

The story

Every team is the founding team of ChaiCart, a 10-minute chai and snack delivery startup. Each topic on the agenda becomes a decision the startup has to make:

  • Day 1, Build: choose a service model, a deployment model, a cloud provider and an architecture.
  • Day 2, Survive: launch day goes wrong. Teams investigate an outage, manage reliability, control costs, connect business systems and pitch at Demo Day.

Refer back to ChaiCart in every explanation: “So what would this mean for ChaiCart?” The story is what makes the concepts stick.

The team → region structure mirrors real cloud design (availability zones inside regions) and lets you run 24 teams without the day dragging: pitches, judging and marking happen inside regions in parallel, and only the region winners come to the main stage.

Mix the semesters

Put 5th- and 7th-semester students in every team (ideally 2–3 of each). Seniors bring project and internship experience; juniors bring energy. Pre-assign teams on a list and project it as students arrive.

Each team starts with 1,000 credits. The official scores live in leaderboard.html, which shows the top three overall and all four regions side by side, and saves automatically in the browser. Use Enter round scores to add a whole activity's results for all 24 teams at once. Show it (press P for presentation mode) at the start of every session.

Your rhythm for every block

  1. Hook (1–2 min): a question, a story or a surprise.
  2. Concept (5–10 min): the analogy slide first, then the technical slide.
  3. Activity (10–30 min): teams play; captains circulate in their regions.
  4. Debrief (5 min): ask the debrief questions before revealing answers.
  5. Score and transition: captains hand in results, the scorekeeper enters them, you read the transition line.

Using the slide decks

  • → / Space: next (reveals items one at a time first). ←: back. F: full screen. Home / End: first / last slide.
  • Activity slides have a timer: click Start. It turns clay-coloured when time is up.
  • Jump to a slide by adding #N to the address, e.g. day2-slides.html#11.
Logistics

Room, regions and captains

Room layout

  • 24 tables of 5, grouped into four quadrants, one per region, with a clear aisle between quadrants (the Paper Plane Factory uses these aisles as flight paths).
  • A stage area at the front with space for 7 people in a line (Human Kitchen, Follow the Order).
  • Wall space for 12 poster stations (two sets of six posters), spaced around the room.
  • Place a table tent on each table before students arrive (cards.html → Table tents).
  • Projector plus two microphones: one for you, one handheld for volunteers and pitches. In a room of 120 a mic is not optional.

People you need

If you can only get two helpers, make them captains for two regions each and do the scorekeeping yourself during breaks. Without any helpers, the region rounds still work: teams vote, and region results are read out by one elected CEO per region.

The region-round pattern

Used for Shark Tank, Architecture Lego, the postmortem and Demo Day:

  1. All 24 teams do the activity at their tables.
  2. Inside each region, teams present briefly to each other (60–90 seconds each) while the captain times.
  3. The region votes (each team one vote, not for itself) or the captain picks the best.
  4. The four region winners present on the main stage. You and the captains choose the overall winner.

This keeps all 24 teams active, takes about the same time as 4 presentations, and gives every region a moment on stage.

How scores reach the leaderboard

  1. Teams keep their own running log on the team sheet (the CFO's job).
  2. Captains write each activity's result for their six teams on the region score sheet.
  3. At the end of each block, the scorekeeper opens Enter round scores, types all 24 results and names the round. One click applies everything, and Undo last reverses the whole round if there is a mistake.
  4. Self-marked activities (quizzes, service sort, treasure hunt, poker, bill shock): each team's COO tells its captain the number; the captain writes it on the sheet.

Awards at Demo Day

  • Richest Startup: top of the overall leaderboard after the final quiz.
  • Region Champions (×4): top team in each region.
  • Sherlock Award: first correct murder mystery solution in the room.
  • Best Architecture and Best Blameless Postmortem: the overall winners of those activities.
  • Most Creative Pitch: from the Demo Day grand final.
Keep scoring visible and generous. With 24 teams, most students watch their region more than the overall table; the region view keeps every team in a race it can win.

Opening, roles and “Cloud or Not?”

Slides 1–6
GoalEnergy up, teams seated, and a first realisation that “cloud” is a way of delivering computing, not a place.
MaterialsTable tents and team sheets on tables; team list projected as students arrive; pre-survey (optional).
SetupCaptains stand in their quadrants and help students find their tables.
  1. Slide 1: pause on the title, then reveal the line.
    For the next two days you are not students. You are founders. You're going to build a company in the cloud, and tomorrow we find out if it survives.
  2. Slide 2: the arc: Build → Survive → Pitch, and the 1,000 credits.
  3. Slide 3: explain teams as availability zones and regions. Introduce the four captains by name and region; ask each region to cheer.
  4. Slide 4: 5-minute timer. Teams assign the five roles on their team sheet.
  5. Slide 5: everyone stands. Reveal items one by one: stand = cloud, sit = not cloud. Hand the mic to one student per item to justify.
  6. Slide 6: reveal the answers. Spend time on the two twists: UPI (a private, regulated platform) and Excel (depends where the file lives).
Debrief
  • “What makes something cloud?” Look for: someone else runs it, network access, shared, pay for what you use.
120 students standing and sitting is loud and fun. Let it be, but keep it to about 8 minutes. The definition comes on the next slide.

Murder mystery: Who Killed Checkout?

Slides 11–15
GoalCorrelate metrics, logs, traces and change events to find a root cause.
Materials24 sealed envelopes (captains hand out 6 each); solution page for each captain.
ScoringSee slide 13; up to +525.
  1. Slide 11: captains give every team its envelope face down. On your word: “Open them. Now.”
  2. Slide 12: introduce the six suspects with some theatre.
  3. Slide 13: rules and scoring. 25-minute timer. COOs hand accusation forms to their captain, who writes the time on each.
  4. Hints (announce to the whole room if most teams are stuck):
    • At 10 min: “Compare database connections before and after 08:58.”
    • At 15 min: “Look at the log lines from two days ago, then today's.”
    • At 20 min: “In the failed trace, what's missing?”
  5. While teams work, captains mark forms as they arrive using the solution page, and tell you the first correct team in their region with its time. The earliest of the four wins the +100 room bonus and the Sherlock Award.
  6. Slide 14, reveal: go suspect by suspect, innocents first. Ask the room to shout “innocent” or “guilty” before each reveal.
  7. Slide 15, lessons: stress the irony: a cost-saving change caused the outage. After the reveal, use the deployed app companion if rehearsed: baseline in Healthy / Roll back, select Deploy Dave: pool 50 → 5, launch the surge, compare waits/504s and missing gateway spans, then roll back and stop traffic. Keep controls hidden during the paper investigation. Live timings will differ from the printed evidence.
The solution in one breath

Deploy Dave (payment-service v2.3.1 at 08:58) cut the DB connection pool from 50 to 5 to save cost. Traffic Tara (3× launch traffic at 09:00) exhausted the five connections. Requests waited 30 s and timed out, and checkout returned 504s. The database, gateway, cache and disk are innocent: the database was starved, not overloaded.

Most teams first accuse Postgres Pete or Traffic Tara. That is the teaching moment: “Traffic was the trigger, but what made the system fragile?” Accept “the 08:58 deployment” as the killer; award weapon points only for the connection-pool explanation.
Break (15 min). “After the break you'll do the same thing in a real tool with real data.”
